Budgeting Basics

A budget is a plan for how you will spend and save your money over a period of
time, usually a month. The core idea is simple: track what comes in (income)
and what goes out (expenses), then make intentional choices instead of
letting money slip away unnoticed.

The 50/30/20 rule is a popular starting framework. Roughly 50% of after-tax
income goes to needs (rent, groceries, utilities, minimum debt payments), 30%
goes to wants (dining out, entertainment, hobbies), and 20% goes to savings
and extra debt repayment. It is a guideline, not a law — adjust the
percentages to fit your situation, especially if you live in a high cost of
living area or are aggressively paying down debt.

Steps to build a simple budget:
1. List all sources of monthly income after tax.
2. List fixed expenses (rent/mortgage, insurance, loan payments, subscriptions).
3. List variable expenses (groceries, transport, entertainment).
4. Subtract total expenses from income. If the number is negative, look for
   variable expenses to trim first, since fixed expenses are harder to change
   quickly.
5. Assign any leftover money a job: emergency fund, debt payoff, retirement
   savings, or a specific goal.

Common budgeting methods:
- Zero-based budgeting: every dollar of income is assigned a job (spend,
  save, or invest) until the total reaches zero.
- Envelope system: cash (or a virtual equivalent) is divided into spending
  categories; when an envelope is empty, spending in that category stops.
- Pay-yourself-first: a fixed percentage of income is automatically moved to
  savings the moment it arrives, before any spending happens.

A budget works best when it is reviewed regularly — many people check in
weekly at first, then monthly once habits stabilize. Expect to adjust the
plan as life changes (a new job, a move, a new expense) rather than treating
it as fixed forever.
