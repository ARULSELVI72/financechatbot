Investing Basics

Investing means putting money into assets — stocks, bonds, funds, real
estate, and others — with the expectation that they will grow in value or
generate income over time, in exchange for accepting some level of risk.

Stocks and bonds:
A stock represents partial ownership in a company; its value rises and falls
with the company's performance and broader market sentiment, offering higher
potential long-term returns alongside higher short-term volatility. A bond is
essentially a loan to a company or government that pays periodic interest;
bonds are generally lower-risk and lower-return than stocks, and are often
used to balance a portfolio.

Index funds and diversification:
An index fund is a fund that holds a broad basket of securities (for
example, all the companies in a stock market index) instead of a small
number of individually picked stocks. This spreads risk across many
companies, so the poor performance of any single company has a limited
effect on the overall investment. Because index funds are not actively
managed by someone picking stocks, they typically have low fees, which
matters a lot over long time horizons since fees compound just like returns
do.

Risk and time horizon:
Generally, money needed within the next few years (a house down payment, an
emergency fund) should not be invested in volatile assets like stocks, since
there may not be time to recover from a downturn before the money is needed.
Money with a longer time horizon (retirement savings decades away) can
typically tolerate more short-term volatility in exchange for potentially
higher long-term growth.

Compound interest:
Compound interest is interest earned on both the original principal and on
previously earned interest. The formula for compound growth is:
    A = P × (1 + r)^t
where P is the principal, r is the interest rate per period (as a decimal),
and t is the number of periods. Because growth compounds, starting to invest
early — even with smaller amounts — tends to matter more than the exact
amount invested per month.

Getting started:
A common on-ramp is contributing to a retirement account (see the retirement
accounts document) using a diversified, low-cost index fund, then
increasing the contribution percentage over time as income grows.
