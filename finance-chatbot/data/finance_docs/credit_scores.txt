Understanding Credit Scores

A credit score is a numeric summary (commonly ranging from about 300 to 850
in common US scoring models) of how risky a lender considers it to lend you
money, based on your credit history.

Main factors that influence a credit score:
1. Payment history (the largest factor) — whether you have paid past bills
   on time. Even a single missed payment reported to a credit bureau can
   have a noticeable negative effect.
2. Credit utilization — how much of your available revolving credit (mainly
   credit cards) you are using. Keeping utilization low, often cited as
   under 30% and ideally lower, tends to help the score.
3. Length of credit history — older accounts generally help, which is why
   closing your oldest credit card can sometimes hurt your score.
4. Credit mix — having a mix of credit types (credit cards, installment
   loans) can have a small positive effect.
5. New credit inquiries — applying for many new credit accounts in a short
   period can temporarily lower a score, since it can look like financial
   distress to a lender.

Practical habits that support a healthy score:
- Pay at least the minimum on every bill, every time, ideally in full to
  avoid interest.
- Keep credit card balances low relative to their limits.
- Avoid closing old accounts unless there is a good reason (like a high
  annual fee with no benefit to you).
- Check your credit report periodically for errors — mistakes do happen, and
  disputing them can improve your score.

Why it matters:
Lenders use credit scores to decide whether to approve loans and credit
cards, and to set the interest rate offered. A stronger score can translate
into meaningfully lower interest costs over the life of a mortgage, auto
loan, or credit card balance, so it is worth treating credit health as an
ongoing habit rather than a one-time fix.
