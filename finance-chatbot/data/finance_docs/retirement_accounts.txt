Retirement Accounts: Traditional vs Roth

Two common tax-advantaged retirement account types (in the US context) are
Traditional and Roth accounts, available as either an employer-sponsored
401(k)/403(b) or an individual IRA.

Traditional accounts:
Contributions are typically made with pre-tax dollars, which lowers taxable
income in the year of the contribution. The money grows tax-deferred, and
withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. This can be
advantageous if you expect to be in a lower tax bracket in retirement than
you are now.

Roth accounts:
Contributions are made with after-tax dollars, so there is no upfront tax
deduction. In exchange, qualified withdrawals in retirement — including all
investment growth — are completely tax-free. This can be advantageous if you
expect to be in a similar or higher tax bracket in retirement, or if you
simply want tax-free income later and the certainty that comes with it.

Employer matching:
Many employers match a percentage of 401(k) contributions up to a limit
(for example, 50% of the first 6% of salary contributed). This match is
essentially free money, so a common piece of guidance is to contribute at
least enough to capture the full employer match before prioritizing other
goals.

Contribution limits:
Retirement accounts have annual contribution limits set by tax authorities,
and these limits change periodically (often increasing slightly each year
to account for inflation). Because these figures change, check current
limits directly with a plan provider or tax authority rather than relying on
a possibly outdated number.

General decision framework:
- Capture any employer match first.
- Decide between Traditional and Roth based on your current vs. expected
  future tax bracket, or split contributions between both for tax
  diversification.
- Increase contribution percentage over time, for example whenever you get a
  raise.
