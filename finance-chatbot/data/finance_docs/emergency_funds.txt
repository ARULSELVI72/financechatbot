Emergency Funds

An emergency fund is money set aside specifically to cover unexpected costs —
a job loss, a medical bill, an urgent car or home repair — without having to
rely on high-interest debt like credit cards.

How much to save:
A common guideline is three to six months of essential living expenses
(rent, utilities, groceries, insurance, minimum debt payments — not
discretionary spending). People with less stable income (freelancers,
commission-based jobs) or dependents often aim for the higher end, or even
nine to twelve months. Someone with a very stable job, a working partner, and
low fixed costs might be comfortable closer to three months.

Where to keep it:
The fund should be liquid (accessible within a day or two) and low-risk,
since the point is safety, not growth. A high-yield savings account is a
common choice because it is separate from everyday checking (reducing the
temptation to spend it) while still earning some interest. Money market
accounts are another option. Investing an emergency fund in stocks is
generally discouraged, since the fund might be needed exactly when markets
are down.

How to build it:
Building a full emergency fund can feel slow. A practical approach is to
start with a smaller milestone — for example, one month of expenses, or even
just $1,000 — before working toward the full three-to-six-month target. Many
people automate a fixed transfer to the fund each payday so it grows without
requiring an active decision every time.

Using it:
An emergency fund is meant to be used for genuine emergencies, not planned
expenses like holiday gifts or annual insurance premiums (those are better
handled with separate "sinking funds" saved for in advance). After an
emergency fund is used, it is worth rebuilding it before ramping up other
financial goals like extra investing.
